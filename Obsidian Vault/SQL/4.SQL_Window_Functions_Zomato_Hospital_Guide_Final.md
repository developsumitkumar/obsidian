# SECTION 1: What is it? (Layman Explanation)

Look at your `zomato_customers` table. It has `total_spent` and `city` for every customer.

Now imagine Zomato's business team asks:
> *"Show me EVERY customer individually, along with their spending rank compared to OTHER customers in the SAME city — but don't hide or merge any customer row, I still want to see all 201 of them."*

A plain `GROUP BY city` would **collapse** all customers in "Mumbai" into a single summary row (like just the average spend) — losing every individual customer's detail. But the business team wants to keep every customer visible AND see a calculated rank next to them.

> **A Window Function lets you look at a "window" (a group) of rows related to the current row, calculate something using that group, and return the result on every individual row — without collapsing anything.**

In simple words: `GROUP BY` gives you **fewer rows** with summaries. A **Window Function gives you the SAME number of rows** (all 201 customers, or all rows in `payment`), but with extra calculated columns added by peeking at related rows.

# SECTION 2: Why do we need it?

## The Business Problem

Take your `zomato_customers` table. The business team asks three real questions:

1. *"Rank every customer by `total_spent`, from highest to lowest, WITHOUT hiding any individual customer."*
2. *"Within each `city`, who are the top spenders? I need this per-city, not just one global ranking."*
3. *"For our loyalty program, identify each customer's spend rank among people who share their `favourite_cuisine` — without losing anyone from the list."*

**Without window functions**, you'd be stuck:
- `GROUP BY city` alone would **destroy customer-level detail** — you'd get one row per city, not one row per customer.
- You'd need painful **self-joins** — e.g., joining `zomato_customers` to itself just to compare "this customer's spend" to "everyone else in the same city" — extremely messy and slow as the table grows beyond 201 rows to millions.
- Calculating something like "each customer's rank within their city" without window functions would require manual looping logic outside the database entirely.

**Window Functions solve this by letting SQL do row-level ranking, running totals, and comparisons natively, in a single clean query — on both your Hospital data AND your Zomato customer data.**

# SECTION 4: Technical Definition

A **Window Function** performs a calculation across a set of rows related to the current row (called a "window"), **without collapsing the result into a single row** like `GROUP BY` does.

**Key terms explained:**

| Term | Simple Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Window** | The group of rows the function is allowed to "look at" for the current row's calculation. |
| **OVER()** | The clause that turns a normal function into a window function, and defines the window. |
| **PARTITION BY** | Splits rows into separate groups (like a mini GROUP BY) — the window function restarts its calculation for each group (e.g., restart per `city`). |
| **ORDER BY (inside OVER)** | Defines the order rows are processed in — critical for ranking, running totals, and LAG/LEAD. |
| **Frame** | A more precise sub-window within the partition (e.g., "only this row and the 2 rows before it") — defined using `ROWS BETWEEN`. |

**Main categories of Window Functions:**

| Category | Functions | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Ranking** | `ROW_NUMBER()`, `RANK()`, `DENSE_RANK()`, `NTILE()` | Assign a position/rank to each row |
| **Aggregate as Window** | `SUM()`, `AVG()`, `COUNT()`, `MIN()`, `MAX()` used with `OVER()` | Running totals, group totals shown per-row |
| **Value/Offset** | `LAG()`, `LEAD()`, `FIRST_VALUE()`, `LAST_VALUE()` | Look at a previous/next/first/last row's value |

# SECTION 5: Architecture (Visual — How a Window Function Processes Rows)

```
   zomato_customers table (rows, unsorted as stored)
   +----+-------------------+--------+-------------+
   | id | name              | city   | total_spent |
   +----+-------------------+--------+-------------+
   | 3  | Gabriel Bryan     | Bangalore |  30427.50 |
   | 1  | Scott Duncan Jr.  | Mumbai    |  50190.20 |
   | 2  | Alan Price        | Chennai   |  44609.00 |
   +----+-------------------+--------+-------------+
                    |
                    v
     SQL applies OVER(PARTITION BY city ORDER BY total_spent DESC)
     -- restarts ranking separately within EACH city --
                    |
                    v
   +----+-------------------+-----------+-------------+---------------+
   | id | name              | city      | total_spent | rank_in_city  |
   +----+-------------------+-----------+-------------+---------------+
   | 1  | Scott Duncan Jr.  | Mumbai    |  50190.20   |      1        |
   | 2  | Alan Price        | Chennai   |  44609.00   |      1        |
   | 3  | Gabriel Bryan     | Bangalore |  30427.50   |      1        |
   +----+-------------------+-----------+-------------+---------------+
     (All original rows STILL present — rank restarts fresh per city)
```

**Component explanation:**
- **Original rows** — every one of the 201 customers stays exactly as is; window functions never delete or merge rows.
- **OVER(...) clause** — tells SQL: "before calculating this function, sort/group the rows this way."
- **PARTITION BY city** — creates a separate mini-window PER CITY — so Mumbai's ranking is completely independent of Chennai's.
- **Result column** — the calculated rank is added as a NEW column, alongside all original customer columns.

# SECTION 6: Step-by-Step — What Happens Internally

**Example query (on your real `zomato_customers` table):**
```sql
SELECT id, name, city, total_spent,
       RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY total_spent DESC) AS spend_rank_in_city
FROM zomato_customers;
```

1. **Step 1:** SQL first runs the base query — selecting all 201 rows from `zomato_customers`.
2. **Step 2:** It detects `OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY total_spent DESC)` and recognizes this as a window function.
3. **Step 3:** SQL logically groups rows by `city` — Mumbai customers together, Delhi customers together, and so on.
4. **Step 4:** WITHIN each city group, SQL sorts customers by `total_spent`, highest first.
5. **Step 5:** For each customer, SQL assigns a rank based on their position within their OWN city's sorted group — the ranking restarts back at 1 for every new city.
6. **Step 6:** This rank is attached to that row as a new column, `spend_rank_in_city`.
7. **Step 7:** The final result has all 201 original rows, each now showing where they stand among customers in their own city.

# SECTION 8: Project Example

###  Project: Zomato City-Wise Loyalty Ranking Dashboard

**Business Requirement:**
Zomato wants to identify the TOP 3 spenders in EVERY city, to send them a special loyalty reward — but the report must still show every single customer's row for reference, not just the top 3.

**Architecture:**
```
zomato_customers table
        |
        v
RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY total_spent DESC)   <- calculates rank on every row
        |
        v
Wrap in CTE, filter WHERE rank <= 3 in outer query           <- keeps only top 3 per city
        |
        v
Loyalty Rewards List (grouped by city, top 3 each)
```

**Data Flow:**
1. Base data already exists in `zomato_customers` — no new tables needed.
2. A single query applies the window function, then an outer query filters to the top 3 per city.

**Challenges:**
- What if two customers in the same city have the EXACT same `total_spent`? Should both get a reward, or only one?
- Some customers have `is_verified = 0` (unverified accounts) — should they be eligible for rewards at all?

**Solution:**
```sql
WITH RankedCustomers AS (
    SELECT id, name, city, total_spent, is_verified,
           DENSE_RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY total_spent DESC) AS spend_rank
    FROM zomato_customers
    WHERE is_verified = 1
)
SELECT * FROM RankedCustomers WHERE spend_rank <= 3;
```
Using `DENSE_RANK()` instead of `RANK()` ensures that if two customers tie for 1st place in a city, BOTH are correctly included, and the next customer is still ranked 2nd (not skipped to 3rd) — a deliberate, defensible business decision.

**Output:**
A clean, per-city Top-3 loyalty list, built from verified customers only, with fair tie-handling — ready to hand to the marketing team.

# SECTION 9: Code Examples (All using your real tables)

##  Beginner Level — ROW_NUMBER() on `zomato_customers`

```sql
SELECT id, name, total_spent,
       ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY total_spent DESC) AS overall_spend_rank
FROM zomato_customers;
```
**Explanation:**
- `ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY total_spent DESC)` gives every customer a unique rank (1, 2, 3...) based on how much they've spent, highest first.
- All 201 customer rows remain — we've simply added a new ranking column, unlike `GROUP BY` which would collapse them.

##  Medium Level — PARTITION BY on `zomato_customers` (city-wise ranking)

```sql
SELECT id, name, city, total_spent,
       RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY total_spent DESC) AS rank_in_city
FROM zomato_customers;
```
**Explanation:**
- `PARTITION BY city` tells SQL: "restart the ranking separately for each city group."
- Since your `zomato_customers` table has MANY customers per city (Mumbai, Chennai, Bangalore, Delhi, Jaipur, etc.), this produces a genuinely useful, repeating ranking pattern — a great table to demonstrate PARTITION BY with, unlike a dataset where every group value is unique.

Try the same idea on Hospital data using `bill_status`:
```sql
SELECT bill_id, patient_id, total_amount, bill_status,
       ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY bill_status ORDER BY total_amount DESC) AS rank_within_status
FROM payment;
```

##  Advanced Level — RANK vs DENSE_RANK + LAG on `zomato_customers`

```sql
SELECT id, name, favourite_cuisine, total_orders,
       RANK()       OVER (PARTITION BY favourite_cuisine ORDER BY total_orders DESC) AS rank_with_gaps,
       DENSE_RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY favourite_cuisine ORDER BY total_orders DESC) AS rank_no_gaps,
       LAG(total_orders) OVER (PARTITION BY favourite_cuisine ORDER BY created_at) AS previous_customer_orders
FROM zomato_customers;
```
**Explanation:**
- `PARTITION BY favourite_cuisine` creates a separate ranking group for each cuisine (Chinese, South Indian, Mughlai, etc.).
- `RANK()` gives tied `total_orders` values the same rank, then **skips** the next number (e.g., 1, 2, 2, 4).
- `DENSE_RANK()` also ties equally, but does **NOT skip** (e.g., 1, 2, 2, 3).
- `LAG(total_orders) OVER (PARTITION BY favourite_cuisine ORDER BY created_at)` looks back at the PREVIOUS customer's order count (within the same cuisine group, ordered by signup date) — the first customer signed up for each cuisine group shows `NULL`, since there's no earlier row to look back at.

```sql
-- Running total of spending on the Hospital payment table
SELECT patient_id, total_amount, bill_date,
       SUM(total_amount) OVER (ORDER BY bill_date) AS running_total
FROM payment
WHERE bill_status = 'Paid';
```
**Explanation:** Filtering `WHERE bill_status = 'Paid'` happens BEFORE the window function runs, so only paid bills contribute to the running total — an important pattern covered further in Section 15 (Common Mistakes).

# SECTION 10: Interview Questions

##  10 Easy

1. **What is a Window Function?**
   *Answer:* A function that performs a calculation across a related set of rows without collapsing them into fewer rows, unlike GROUP BY. *Why asked:* Foundational conceptual check.
2. **What does the OVER() clause do?**
   *Answer:* It defines the "window" (the group and order of rows) that the function should calculate across. *Why asked:* Core syntax understanding.
3. **What is the difference between ROW_NUMBER() and RANK()?**
   *Answer:* ROW_NUMBER() always gives unique sequential numbers even for ties; RANK() gives the same number to tied rows and then skips the next number. *Why asked:* One of the most common SQL interview questions overall.
4. **What does PARTITION BY do inside OVER()?**
   *Answer:* It splits data into separate groups (e.g., by `city`), and the window function's calculation restarts for each group — similar to GROUP BY, but without collapsing rows. *Why asked:* Core concept check.
5. **Can you use ORDER BY inside the OVER() clause?**
   *Answer:* Yes — it controls the order in which rows are processed, essential for ranking and running totals. *Why asked:* Basic syntax awareness.
6. **What is the main difference between GROUP BY and a Window Function?**
   *Answer:* GROUP BY reduces multiple rows into one summary row per group (e.g., one row per city); Window Functions keep every original customer row while adding a calculated column. *Why asked:* THE most fundamental distinction tested in every interview.
7. **Name two ranking window functions.**
   *Answer:* ROW_NUMBER() and RANK() (also DENSE_RANK()). *Why asked:* Basic recall.
8. **Can normal aggregate functions like SUM() be used as window functions?**
   *Answer:* Yes, by adding OVER() after them, e.g., SUM(total_spent) OVER (...). *Why asked:* Tests awareness that window functions extend familiar aggregates.
9. **What does LAG() do?**
   *Answer:* It retrieves a value from a previous row (based on the specified order), useful for row-to-row comparisons. *Why asked:* Common practical function.
10. **What does LEAD() do?**
    *Answer:* It retrieves a value from a following/next row — the opposite direction of LAG(). *Why asked:* Common practical function, paired with LAG().

##  10 Medium

1. **What's the difference between RANK() and DENSE_RANK() when there are ties?**
   *Answer:* RANK() leaves gaps after ties (1,2,2,4); DENSE_RANK() does not (1,2,2,3). *Why asked:* Common trick question testing precise understanding.
2. **What does NTILE(4) do?**
   *Answer:* It divides the result set into 4 roughly equal groups/buckets and assigns each row a bucket number — useful for creating spending quartiles across your 201 Zomato customers. *Why asked:* Tests knowledge beyond the "big 3" ranking functions.
3. **Can you use a Window Function in a WHERE clause directly?**
   *Answer:* No — window functions are evaluated AFTER WHERE, so you must wrap the query in a subquery/CTE and filter in the OUTER query instead. *Why asked:* Extremely common real-world gotcha.
4. **What is the default window frame when ORDER BY is used without an explicit frame?**
   *Answer:* "RANGE BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW" by default — including all rows from the start of the partition up to (and including) the current row. *Why asked:* Tests deeper understanding of window frame defaults.
5. **How would you find each city's SINGLE top-spending customer using a window function?**
   *Answer:* Use `RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY total_spent DESC)` in a CTE, then filter `WHERE rank = 1` in the outer query. *Why asked:* Very common "top-N per group" real-world pattern.
6. **What's the difference between COUNT(*) OVER() with no PARTITION BY, versus with PARTITION BY city?**
   *Answer:* Without PARTITION BY, it returns the total customer count (201) on every row; with PARTITION BY city, it returns the count of customers only in that row's own city, shown on every row of that city group. *Why asked:* Tests understanding of partition scope.
7. **How is FIRST_VALUE() different from MIN()?**
   *Answer:* MIN() returns the smallest value regardless of row order; FIRST_VALUE() returns the value of the FIRST row per the specified ORDER BY, which may not be the smallest value at all. *Why asked:* Subtle but important distinction.
8. **Why might LAG() return NULL for the first customer in a city partition?**
   *Answer:* Because there's no "previous" row before the first row within that partition — LAG() has nothing to look back at. *Why asked:* Common practical edge case.
9. **How would you calculate a 3-row moving average of `total_spent` ordered by `created_at`?**
   *Answer:* `AVG(total_spent) OVER (ORDER BY created_at ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)` — averages the current row plus the 2 rows before it. *Why asked:* Tests understanding of custom window frames.
10. **Can you PARTITION BY multiple columns at once?**
    *Answer:* Yes, e.g., `PARTITION BY city, favourite_cuisine` — creates a separate window for every unique combination of both columns. *Why asked:* Tests practical multi-dimensional grouping knowledge.

##  10 Advanced

1. **Explain the exact order of SQL clause execution and why window functions can't be used directly in WHERE.**
   *Answer:* Logical execution order: FROM → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → SELECT (window functions calculated here) → ORDER BY. Since window functions are calculated during/after SELECT, their results don't exist yet when WHERE runs — hence the need for a subquery/CTE wrapper. *Why asked:* Tests deep understanding of SQL's internal execution model.
2. **What is the difference between ROWS and RANGE in a window frame definition?**
   *Answer:* ROWS defines the frame by physical row position; RANGE defines it by logical VALUE (matters especially when multiple customers share the exact same `total_spent`). *Why asked:* Very advanced, tests precise frame semantics.
3. **How would you find, for each city, the customer(s) with the highest `total_orders`, handling ties fairly?**
   *Answer:* Use `DENSE_RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY total_orders DESC)`, then filter `WHERE rnk = 1` — DENSE_RANK ensures multiple tied customers are all correctly returned, unlike ROW_NUMBER which would arbitrarily pick just one. *Why asked:* Real business logic decision test.
4. **How do window functions perform compared to correlated subqueries for the same task, and why?**
   *Answer:* Window functions compute in a single optimized pass over sorted/partitioned data; correlated subqueries often re-scan the table once PER ROW, which becomes very slow as the table grows well beyond 201 rows. *Why asked:* Performance-focused senior-level question.
5. **How would you remove exact duplicate customer records (e.g., same email appearing twice) using a window function?**
   *Answer:* Use `ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY email ORDER BY created_at ASC)` in a CTE, then keep/delete rows where the row number is greater than 1. *Why asked:* Extremely common real-world data-cleaning pattern, directly applicable to a messy customer table like this one.
6. **Explain how you'd calculate a running total of `total_spent` that RESETS per `state`, ordered by `created_at`.**
   *Answer:* `SUM(total_spent) OVER (PARTITION BY state ORDER BY created_at)` — PARTITION BY ensures the running total restarts fresh for every state group. *Why asked:* Tests correct combination of PARTITION BY + ORDER BY.
7. **What happens if you use SUM() with PARTITION BY but WITHOUT ORDER BY — how does that differ from including ORDER BY?**
   *Answer:* Without ORDER BY, SUM() returns the FULL partition total on every row (e.g., total spend for the whole city, repeated on every customer's row). WITH ORDER BY, the default frame changes to "start of partition to current row," producing a running total instead. *Why asked:* Extremely important, frequently misunderstood nuance.
8. **How would you flag customers whose `last_login` is unusually far from their `created_at` (signup date), possibly indicating inactive/dormant accounts?**
   *Answer:* This isn't a classic LAG/LEAD case since it compares two columns on the SAME row, not neighboring rows — but window functions could still help rank customers by "days since last login" per city using `RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY (last_login - created_at))` to prioritize re-engagement campaigns regionally. *Why asked:* Tests applying window functions creatively to a real business problem, not just memorized patterns.
9. **How would you find the customer with the SECOND highest `total_spent` overall, using a window function instead of LIMIT/OFFSET?**
   *Answer:* Wrap `DENSE_RANK() OVER (ORDER BY total_spent DESC)` in a CTE, then filter `WHERE rnk = 2` in the outer query — correctly handles ties, unlike simple LIMIT/OFFSET. *Why asked:* Classic "Nth highest value" question.
10. **In a Databricks/Spark environment, are window functions computed differently than in a traditional single-node RDBMS?**
    *Answer:* Conceptually identical logic, but in Spark, PARTITION BY also affects how data is SHUFFLED across worker nodes — a window function without PARTITION BY can force ALL data onto a single node, a serious bottleneck at big-data scale (imagine this customer table scaled to 200 million rows instead of 201). *Why asked:* Tests bridge between SQL theory and real Data Engineering platform behavior.

# SECTION 14: Scenario-Based Questions

1. **"Marketing wants to see EVERY customer individually, but also wants each customer ranked against others in the SAME city. A simple GROUP BY collapses too much detail — what do you do?"**
   *Ideal solution:* Use `RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY total_spent DESC)` — preserves every customer row while adding the rank column scoped to each city.

2. **"You need the running total of `total_spent` across all customers over time (by `created_at`), but only for `is_verified = 1` customers."**
   *Ideal solution:* Filter with `WHERE is_verified = 1` FIRST (before the window function logically runs), then apply `SUM(total_spent) OVER (ORDER BY created_at)`.

3. **"You're asked to find the customer with the MOST `total_orders` in each `state`, but there might be ties."**
   *Ideal solution:* Use `DENSE_RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY state ORDER BY total_orders DESC)` so ties are both correctly returned, instead of `ROW_NUMBER()` which would arbitrarily drop one.

4. **"The business team wants only the customer with the SECOND highest `total_spent` overall."**
   *Ideal solution:* Use `DENSE_RANK() OVER (ORDER BY total_spent DESC)` inside a CTE, then filter `WHERE rnk = 2` in the outer query — safer than LIMIT/OFFSET since it handles tied spend correctly.

5. **"You want to compare each customer's `total_spent` to the customer who signed up right before them, within the same `city`."**
   *Ideal solution:* Use `total_spent - LAG(total_spent) OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY created_at)` and inspect the resulting differences.

6. **"The hospital's finance team wants to know which bills in `payment` did NOT get marked 'Paid', without losing any bill from the report."**
   *Ideal solution:* This can be answered with a simple filter, but if combined with ranking (e.g., "rank unpaid bills by size"), use `RANK() OVER (ORDER BY total_amount DESC)` applied AFTER filtering `WHERE bill_status != 'Paid'`.

7. **"You need to identify possible duplicate customer signups in `zomato_customers` where the same `email` appears more than once, keeping only the earliest record."**
   *Ideal solution:* Use `ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY email ORDER BY created_at ASC)` in a CTE, then keep only rows where the row number equals 1.

8. **"Management wants a 3-signup moving average of `total_spent` (ordered by `created_at`) to smooth out day-to-day variation in customer value."**
   *Ideal solution:* Apply `AVG(total_spent) OVER (ORDER BY created_at ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)`.

9. **"You suspect some `patient_id` values might have unusually large jumps in insurance `coverage_amount` compared to others with the same `insurance_provider`."**
   *Ideal solution:* Use `RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY insurance_provider ORDER BY coverage_amount DESC)` to surface unusually high values within each provider group for review.

10. **"A loyalty report needs each customer's spend ranked within their own `favourite_cuisine` group, but the report must ONLY show rank 1 (top spender per cuisine)."**
    *Ideal solution:* Compute `RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY favourite_cuisine ORDER BY total_spent DESC)` inside a CTE/subquery, then filter `WHERE rnk = 1` in the outer query (remember: can't filter directly in WHERE on the same-level window function).

# SECTION 15: Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's a problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Trying to filter a window function result directly in WHERE | SQL errors out — window functions aren't available at WHERE-evaluation time | Wrap in a CTE/subquery, filter in the outer query |
| Confusing ROW_NUMBER() with RANK() for tie-handling on `total_spent` | Produces wrong results when duplicate spend values exist (common in real customer data) | Use RANK()/DENSE_RANK() when ties should share a rank |
| Forgetting PARTITION BY city when ranking should reset per city | Ranking accumulates across the ENTIRE customer base instead of per city | Add `PARTITION BY city` |
| Assuming SUM() OVER(PARTITION BY city) without ORDER BY gives a running total | It actually gives the flat total for that WHOLE city on every row, not cumulative | Add `ORDER BY` if a running (cumulative) total is intended |
| Not handling NULLs from LAG()/LEAD() on the first customer per city | Comparisons like `total_spent - LAG(total_spent)` silently produce NULL for the first row in each partition | This is expected behavior — handle with `COALESCE()` if a default is needed |
| Ranking ALL customers (including unverified ones) when business rules require only verified customers | Skews loyalty/reward calculations with ineligible accounts | Filter with `WHERE is_verified = 1` BEFORE the window function runs |

# SECTION 16: Best Practices

- ✅ **Always decide: do I need PARTITION BY?** — if calculations should reset per group (per city, per cuisine), don't forget it.
- ✅ **Prefer DENSE_RANK() over ROW_NUMBER()** when ties should be treated equally (e.g., "top spender" reports on real customer data, which often has genuine ties).
- ✅ **Wrap window function queries in a CTE** when you need to filter on the calculated column.
- ✅ **Be explicit with frames** (`ROWS BETWEEN...`) when doing moving averages or custom windows.
- ✅ **Filter unwanted rows (like unverified customers) BEFORE applying the window function** whenever possible.
- ✅ **Test window function results on a manageable dataset first** (like your 201-row `zomato_customers` table) before scaling to millions of rows.

**Why companies follow these:** Window function bugs are often silent — a query can run successfully but produce subtly wrong business numbers (like the wrong "top spender" per city), which can go unnoticed for a long time and mislead real marketing/finance decisions.

# SECTION 17: Performance Tips

| Technique | When to Use |
|---|---|
| **Filter rows with WHERE before the window function runs** | Always, when some rows are irrelevant (e.g., exclude unverified customers) |
| **Index PARTITION BY / ORDER BY columns** | When window functions run repeatedly on large tables (e.g., `city`, `created_at`) |
| **Avoid unnecessary multiple window functions with different PARTITION BY in one query** | Each distinct window specification may require a separate sort/pass — consolidate where possible |
| **Use explicit ROWS frames instead of default RANGE frames when performance matters** | ROWS frames are often computed more efficiently, especially on large ordered datasets |
| **In Spark/Databricks, always PARTITION BY a meaningful column** | Prevents forcing the entire dataset onto a single executor/node — imagine this customer table at 200 million rows, not 201 |

# SECTION 18: Comparison Table

| Concept A | Concept B | Key Difference |
|---|---|---|
| **GROUP BY** | **Window Function (OVER)** | GROUP BY collapses rows into summaries (e.g., one row per city); Window Functions keep every customer row and add a calculated column |
| **ROW_NUMBER()** | **RANK()** | ROW_NUMBER always unique, even for ties; RANK gives ties the same number and skips the next |
| **RANK()** | **DENSE_RANK()** | RANK leaves gaps after ties; DENSE_RANK does not |
| **LAG()** | **LEAD()** | LAG looks at the previous row; LEAD looks at the next row |
| **PARTITION BY** | **GROUP BY** | PARTITION BY groups for a window calculation while keeping all rows; GROUP BY groups and collapses rows |
| **ROWS frame** | **RANGE frame** | ROWS uses physical row position; RANGE uses logical value-based grouping (matters with ties) |
| **Correlated Subquery** | **Window Function** | Correlated subqueries often re-scan per row (slow); window functions compute in one optimized pass (faster) |

# SECTION 19: Cheat Sheet 📝

```
WINDOW FUNCTION = calculation across related rows, WITHOUT collapsing them

Syntax:
<function>(<column>) OVER (
    [PARTITION BY column1, column2, ...]
    [ORDER BY column3, ...]
    [ROWS/RANGE BETWEEN ... AND ...]
)

Ranking:
ROW_NUMBER()  -> unique sequential number, no ties
RANK()        -> ties share rank, gaps after
DENSE_RANK()  -> ties share rank, NO gaps
NTILE(n)      -> splits rows into n buckets (e.g., spending quartiles)

Aggregate as Window:
SUM()/AVG()/COUNT()/MIN()/MAX()  OVER (...)
  - No ORDER BY  -> flat group total on every row
  - With ORDER BY -> running/cumulative total by default

Offset:
LAG(col)   -> previous row's value
LEAD(col)  -> next row's value
FIRST_VALUE(col) / LAST_VALUE(col)  -> first/last in window per ORDER BY

Golden Rule:
Can't filter window function result in WHERE directly
--> wrap in CTE/subquery, filter in outer query
```

# SECTION 20: Revision Notes (Quick Interview Recall)

-  Window Functions calculate across a group of rows WITHOUT collapsing them (unlike GROUP BY).
-  OVER() is what makes a function a "window function."
-  PARTITION BY = grouping without collapsing (e.g., per city); ORDER BY (inside OVER) = processing/ranking order.
-  ROW_NUMBER = always unique; RANK = gaps after ties; DENSE_RANK = no gaps after ties.
-  SUM() OVER (ORDER BY ...) with no explicit frame = running total by default.
-  LAG/LEAD = look backward/forward to a neighboring row; first/last rows in a partition return NULL if nothing to reference.
-  Window functions CANNOT be filtered directly in WHERE — use a CTE/subquery + outer filter instead.
-  Window functions are generally faster than correlated subqueries for the same row-comparison tasks.
-  In Spark/Databricks, PARTITION BY also impacts data shuffling across nodes — not just logical grouping.

# SECTION 21: Memory Tricks 

- **"GROUP BY shrinks, Window WATCHES"** — GROUP BY reduces rows; Window Functions just "watch" and annotate without shrinking.
- **"RANK skips, DENSE doesn't"** — quick way to recall the tie-handling difference.
- **"LAG = Look behind, LEAD = Look ahead"** — directional word association.
- **"OVER() is the window's blueprint"** — PARTITION BY (which city/group), ORDER BY (which order to walk through), Frame (how far to look).
- **"Can't WHERE a window"** — a short phrase to remember you must wrap in a CTE to filter ranked results.

# SECTION 24: Summary

Window Functions let SQL perform calculations — rankings, running totals, and row-to-row comparisons — across a group ("window") of related rows, **without collapsing them** the way `GROUP BY` does. Every original row stays visible, with a new calculated column added alongside it. The `OVER()` clause turns an ordinary function into a window function, and inside it, `PARTITION BY` defines separate groups (like restarting a ranking per `city` in your Zomato data, or per `patient_id` in your Hospital data), while `ORDER BY` controls the sequence rows are processed in — critical for ranking and running totals.

The three ranking functions — `ROW_NUMBER()` (always unique), `RANK()` (ties share a rank, then skip), and `DENSE_RANK()` (ties share a rank, no skip) — are among the most frequently tested SQL interview concepts, and your real `zomato_customers` data (with genuine ties in `total_spent` and repeated `city`/`favourite_cuisine` groups) makes these differences concretely visible rather than theoretical. Offset functions `LAG()` and `LEAD()` let you compare a row to its neighbor without a self-join, while aggregate functions like `SUM()`, `AVG()`, and `COUNT()` become powerful running-total or group-total tools the moment `OVER()` is attached. A critical, frequently-tested rule: window function results **cannot be filtered directly in a `WHERE` clause** — they must be wrapped in a CTE or subquery, with the filter applied in an outer query.
